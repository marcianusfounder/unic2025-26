Section one: Culture shock

The term “culture shock” describes a range of emotions that many people experience when they travel abroad and come in contact with an unfamiliar way of life. These feelings include curiosity, confusion, and homesickness. Language barriers and physical disorientation can increase the intensity of some of the more negative emotions.

### Tabela

The Canadian anthropologist Kalervo Oberg suggested that culture shock should be divided into four phases. These phases are often called the honeymoon stage, the crisis stage, the adjustment stage, and the adaptation stage | The adjustment stage typically begins between six months and a year of being in a new place. People begin to accept the differences between the host culture and their own. They start to overcome some of the challenges faced regarding customs and language. While still difficult, things tend to become more manageable on a day-to-day basis.
The honeymoon stage is a period of excitement and interest in the new culture. During this phase, people wish to explore their surroundings and interact with locals. They enjoy the sense of adventure their journey has brought them. | Life gets easier as people enter the adaptation stage and become more integrated into the new culture. By this point, they have made new friends and gotten used to new routines. Frustrations may still arise, but the newcomers realize they don’t need to understand everything to live a balanced and meaningful life.
During the crisis stage, the positive feelings of the honeymoon phase begin to fade as more negative emotions associated with culture shock start to emerge. The crisis stage usually begins about three months after arrival. Things that were at first intriguing about the new culture may now seem strange and frustrating. | People who return to their own country after a long time away may experience reverse culture shock. They often expect to come back to a place that feels unfamiliar, but instead are surprised to discover many things have changed. Furthermore, they are not the same person they once were before they left. Friends and family members may not know how to respond to their attitudes, values, and habits, and it might take time to rebuild relationships. Understanding that reverse culture shock is real can help with the transition, but that doesn’t mean that it’ll be easy.
